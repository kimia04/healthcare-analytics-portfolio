Patterns and Trends in U.S. Healthcare Spending (1996–2016) by Payer, Age, Sex, Care Type, and Disease¶

The University of Washington's Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) evaluates and compiles healthcare data to support and inform healthcare policy and care. Its Disease Expenditure Project analyzes U.S. healthcare spending and provides estimates for 154 conditions across 14 categories, providing insight into spending across public insurance, private insurance, and out-of-pocket payments, as well as health conditions, age group, sex, and type of care.

This dataset incorporates information from government budgets, insurance claims, facility records, household surveys, and other official U.S. sources from 1996-2016. It was released on March 18, 2020, accessed via Kaggle, and downloaded for analysis.

Data Preparation¶

The dataset, containing U.S. healthcare spending from 1996 to 2016, was first loaded and cleaned to facilitate analysis. Irrelevant columns were dropped, and key columns were renamed for clarity: year, age, care_type, and cause. Combined totals for age and sex were removed to avoid double-counting, leaving only individual age and sex groups.

Next, categorical variables were recoded to numeric levels for easier plotting and analysis. Age groups were renumbered sequentially, while care types and causes were also assigned numeric codes. Finally, the dataset was checked for missing values, ensuring the dataset was ready for exploratory data analysis.

Analysis¶

To prepare the data for analysis by payer type, the dataset was reshaped from wide to long format using a melt operation. The mean_pub, mean_pri, and mean_oop columns, representing public, private, and out-of-pocket spending, were combined into a single spending column with an associated payer identifier. The numeric payer codes were mapped to descriptive labels (“Public,” “Private,” and “Out-of-pocket”) for clarity. Additionally, CI was created to store the lower and upper bounds of the confidence interval as a formatted string; however, these intervals correspond to total spending (mean_all) rather than the individual types of payers. This providing an approximate visual representation of uncertainty in spending estimates.

df_melt = df_clean.melt(
    id_vars=['year', 'sex', 'age', 'age_group_name', 'care_type', 'type_of_care', 
             'cause', 'cause_name', 'lower_all', 'upper_all'],
    value_vars=['mean_pub', 'mean_pri', 'mean_oop'],
    var_name='payer',
    value_name='spending'
)

df_melt['payer'] = df_melt['payer'].map({
    'mean_pub': 'Public', 
    'mean_pri': 'Private', 
    'mean_oop': 'Out-of-pocket'
})

df_melt = df_melt[df_melt['sex'] != 'Both']

df_melt['CI'] = df_melt.apply(lambda row: f"{row['lower_all']} – {row['upper_all']}", axis=1)

To summarize spending and associated uncertainty across different groupings, a custom ci_table function was created. This function aggregates the reshaped dataset (df_melt) by one or more grouping variables, such as year, payer, age, sex, care type, or cause. For each group, it calculates the mean spending and retrieves a single confidence interval (CI) value from the first row of that group. When descriptive labels are preferred, numeric codes are replaced with human-readable names for age groups, care types, and causes. The function is then applied to several combinations of grouping variables, producing tables that provide a concise overview of average spending and approximate confidence intervals across key dimensions of the dataset.

These aggregated tables highlight clear patterns in healthcare spending across key dimensions. Over time, spending generally increases for all payer types, with private and public spending consistently higher than out-of-pocket costs. Differences between sexes are apparent, with female spending generally lower than male or combined totals. Spending also rises with age, particularly for private and public payers, while out-of-pocket expenditures remain comparatively lower; males tend to have slightly higher spending than females across most age groups. By type of care, ambulatory, inpatient, and dental services account for the largest average expenditures, whereas nursing and emergency care are lower. Finally, examining spending by cause shows the highest expenditures for cardiovascular disease, musculoskeletal disorders, diabetes, and injuries, with lower spending for communicable diseases and liver conditions.

# confident interval function
def ci_table(group_cols, use_labels=True):
    col_mapping = {
        'age': 'age_group_name',
        'care_type': 'type_of_care',
        'cause': 'cause_name'
    }
    cols_to_use = [col_mapping.get(c, c) if use_labels else c for c in group_cols]
    
    table = df_melt.groupby(cols_to_use).agg(
        mean_spending=('spending','mean'),
        CI=('CI','first')
    ).reset_index()
    
    print(f"\nConfidence Intervals for {group_cols}:\n", table)

# generate confidence interval tables

These figures complement the summary tables by providing an intuitive, visual perspective on spending dynamics, allowing for easier identification of temporal trends, disparities between subgroups, and variation in expenditures across different types of care and health conditions.

Conclusions and Discussion¶

The analysis of U.S. healthcare spending from 1996 to 2016 highlights several clear patterns across payer types, age groups, sexes, care types, and disease categories. Overall, total healthcare spending has increased over time, with private and public expenditures consistently exceeding out-of-pocket payments. Line plots and confidence interval tables both illustrate this upward trend, particularly among older age groups, suggesting that the financial burden of healthcare grows with age and may disproportionately affect public and private payers. Out-of-pocket spending remains comparatively low across most categories, reflecting the structure of insurance coverage and cost-sharing in the U.S. healthcare system.

Age and sex differences are also evident. Spending generally rises with age, with the highest expenditures observed in older adults for private and public payers. Although male spending is slightly higher than female spending in most age groups, some variation exists across years, possibly reflecting differences in disease prevalence, care utilization, or insurance coverage between sexes.

Analysis by care type shows that ambulatory, inpatient, and dental services account for the largest portions of spending, while emergency and nursing care are lower on average. Cause-specific analyses reveal that cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, diabetes, and injuries dominate spending, consistent with the high prevalence and treatment costs of these conditions. Communicable and liver diseases remain lower in overall expenditure, likely due to lower incidence and more targeted treatment requirements.

Taken together, these findings provide a comprehensive view of healthcare expenditure patterns, highlighting major areas of financial impact and potential policy focus. Subsequent visualizations, such as line plots and bar charts, further illustrate trends over time and across subgroups, allowing for clearer identification of changes in spending patterns and payer responsibilities. These insights can inform policy discussions on resource allocation, preventive care, and the design of interventions aimed at reducing cost burdens for both individuals and the healthcare system.
